What this builds on

Almost none of this is new, including the parts that look new. The scoring theory is seventy years old, the reject option fifty, and probability-scored exams were built and tested on students in the 1960s. The last section says how small each remaining step actually is.

By Ramon Gonzalez · Created 2026-08-16 11:10 ET · Last updated 2026-09-03 10:00 ETContent 2026-08-17 01:24 ET · Style 2026-09-03 10:00 ET · Organization 2026-09-03 10:00 ET

The maths was finished before the problem existed

If you want somebody's honest probability, pay them in a way that makes honesty their best bet. Brier wrote down such a payment scheme for weather forecasters in 1950, Good added the logarithmic version two years later, and by 1971 Savage had characterised the entire family of rules with that property. A rule is proper when truthfully reporting your belief maximises your expected score, and strictly proper when nothing else ties it. That is a theorem. It does not care whether the forecaster is a person, a barometer or a language model.

The part that matters for benchmarks is what the theorem excludes. One point for right and zero for wrong is a perfectly reasonable way to rank people who all answer everything, and a strictly improper way to elicit a probability: under it, the best move at any belief above chance is to state your most likely answer with full confidence. Guessing is not a defect in the model. It is the optimal play, and it was known to be the optimal play decades before there were models.

  • Brier (1950), Verification of forecasts expressed in terms of probability, Monthly Weather Review 78:1-3. The quadratic rule, invented so weather forecasters could not hedge for score.
  • Good (1952), Rational decisions, JRSS-B 14:107-114. The logarithmic rule.
  • de Finetti (1962), Does it make sense to speak of “good probability appraisers”? The elicitation question, asked plainly, in one essay.
  • Savage (1971), Elicitation of personal probabilities and expectations, JASA 66:783-801. The characterisation of the whole proper family, which is what makes “use a proper rule” a finite instruction rather than a taste.
  • Murphy (1973), A new vector partition of the probability score, J. Applied Meteorology 12:595-600. Reliability minus resolution plus uncertainty. Why a Brier score can be split into “is calibrated” and “knows things”, which is the decomposition this project reports.
  • Selten (1998), Axiomatic characterization of the quadratic scoring rule, Experimental Economics 1:43-62. The axioms behind the specific rule used here.
  • Gneiting & Raftery (2007), Strictly proper scoring rules, prediction, and estimation, JASA 102:359-378. The modern reference; start here if you start anywhere.
  • Gneiting, Balabdaoui & Raftery (2007), Probabilistic forecasts, calibration and sharpness, JRSS-B 69:243-268. Maximise sharpness subject to calibration. The goal of the whole exercise in six words.

Choosing among proper rules, which has been done carefully twice

Properness narrows the field but does not pick a member of it, and the question of which proper rule has had two serious efforts, sixty years apart, in fields that do not read each other.

The first was psychometrics, and it went further than this project has. de Finetti proposed in 1965 that students answer multiple-choice items by distributing probability across the options and be scored so that honest distribution maximises the mark. Shuford, Albert and Massengill gave the necessary and sufficient condition the following year, along with a recipe for generating an unlimited family of such scoring systems. Others then built the exams and ran them on real students. So the design being proposed here for models was proposed for people six decades ago, tested, and mostly abandoned for the practical reasons the SAT section describes.

The second was decision analysis, and it is the part with a live objection in it. Bickel compared the quadratic, spherical and logarithmic rules and concluded that the logarithmic rule is better when rank order is what you care about, which is exactly what a leaderboard is. Merkle and Steyvers then showed that the common belief that the choice barely matters comes from looking at only the two or three popular rules, and that across a wider family the conclusions move a great deal. This project uses the quadratic rule anyway, and owes a reason: the logarithmic rule is unbounded below, so a single confident error scores minus infinity. On a benchmark where a parse failure or a grader slip can produce exactly that, one bad item would sink a model's entire score, and a metric that fragile will not be adopted no matter how well it behaves in theory. Selten's axioms and the reliability-resolution split are the positive case; boundedness is the one that decides it. Both rules are implemented here and the comparison is free, which is the honest way to hold that position.

  • de Finetti (1965), Methods for discriminating levels of partial knowledge concerning a test item, British J. Mathematical and Statistical Psychology 18:87-123. This project's design, proposed for students, sixty years early.
  • Shuford, Albert & Massengill (1966), Admissible probability measurement procedures, Psychometrika 31:125-145. The necessary and sufficient condition, plus a generator for the whole admissible family. Properness for exams, settled.
  • Kansup & Hakstian (1975), A comparison of several methods of assessing partial knowledge in multiple-choice tests, J. Educational Measurement. Somebody then ran it on real students, which is the step this project is still owed.
  • Bickel (2007), Some comparisons among quadratic, spherical, and logarithmic scoring rules, Decision Analysis 4:49-65. The strongest technical objection to the choice made here: for rank ordering, which is what a leaderboard does, he finds the logarithmic rule superior.
  • Merkle & Steyvers (2013), Choosing a strictly proper scoring rule, Decision Analysis 10:292-304. The choice matters more than the field assumes, because the field has only compared two or three rules.
  • Bröcker & Smith (2007), Scoring probabilistic forecasts: the importance of being proper, Weather and Forecasting 22:382-388; Jose, Nau & Winkler (2008), Scoring rules, generalized entropy, and utility maximization, Operations Research 56:1146-1157. Where the parametric families and their tradeoffs live.

The reject option: when saying nothing is the right move

Chow's 1970 result is the cleanest statement of what a benchmark is missing. Give a classifier the option to say nothing, attach a cost to that option, and the optimal policy falls straight out: answer when your confidence exceeds the ratio of what an error costs to what silence costs. There is no free parameter left to argue about beyond that ratio, and the ratio is not a statistical quantity. It is a claim about the deployment. The single knob in this project's rule, the price of making a claim, is Chow's ratio wearing different clothes.

Everything since is variations on the same move. Selective classification traces the risk-coverageA curve plotting error rate against the fraction of questions a model actually answered, traced as the abstention threshold moves. It is how two models that decline different amounts can be compared at all. curve, showing how much accuracy you buy by answering less often. Conformal prediction goes the dual route and returns a set that provably contains the answer at a chosen rate, so uncertainty appears as the size of the set rather than as a number bolted onto a guess. Both treat abstention as a first-class output. Benchmarks kept the single-answer interface.

  • Chow (1970), On optimum recognition error and reject tradeoff, IEEE Trans. Information Theory 16:41-46. The threshold is a cost ratio, and the cost ratio is somebody's decision.
  • El-Yaniv & Wiener (2010), On the foundations of noise-free selective classification, JMLR 11:1605-1641; Geifman & El-Yaniv (2017), Selective classification for deep neural networks, NeurIPS. Risk-coverage curves, then the same idea for modern nets.
  • Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer (2005), Algorithmic learning in a random world; Angelopoulos & Bates (2023) for the readable introduction. Conformal prediction: the honest alternative that answers with a set instead of a probability.

Human testing ran this experiment, and mis-set the price

The strongest objection this design has to survive does not come from machine learning. For decades the SAT subtracted a quarter point for a wrong answer on a five-choice question, and the College Board removed it in 2016. The usual reading is that pricing error was tried and failed. The sharper reading is that the price was set at the one value that guarantees failure.

A quarter point off out of five choices makes a blind guess worth exactly zero, which is exactly what a blank is worth. At that price the answer-or-abstain decision has no strictly correct answer: a student who knows nothing is indifferent, so what they do is settled by appetite for risk rather than by knowledge. One step above blind, having ruled out a single option, guessing is already worth +0.06 and abstaining is simply wrong. So a rule advertised as a penalty for guessing in fact rewarded guessing nearly everywhere, and the students it cost were the ones who took the instruction at face value. Formula scoring did not measure knowledge worse because it priced error. It measured knowledge worse because the price left the decision indeterminate at the bottom and contradicted its own name above that.

The design that survives moves the price off that point and pays for silence rather than only fining noise. The AMC gives 6 for a correct answer, 0 for a wrong one and 1.5 for a blank, against a blind guess worth 1.2. Declining now strictly beats guessing, the indifference point sits at a belief of one in four rather than at chance, and so there is a right move at every level of knowledge and the instruction describes what the incentive actually does. Nobody argues about it. Kalai et al. reach the same place from the other end: the exams that do price error, JEE, NEET, GATE, the AMC, and the SAT, AP and GRE in earlier years, all state the grading rule in the instructions, and takers know roughly where the threshold sits.

Two things carry across. The price of a claim has to be set so the answer-or-abstain decision has a strict optimum at every belief, which is why this design pays for abstention rather than only penalising error. And the announcement has to be something the reader can act on: a contract whose name disagrees with its own arithmetic is the failure mode the SAT actually shipped, and it is why the wording of every contract here is tested against a simulated respondent before any money is spent on a real one.

  • Budescu & Bar-Hillel (1993), To guess or not to guess: a decision-theoretic view of formula scoring, J. Educational Measurement 30:277-291. The case against, and the reason number-right scoring won. Read this before proposing a penalty.
  • Bar-Hillel, Budescu & Attali (2005), Scoring and keying multiple choice tests: a case study in irrationality, Mind & Society 4:3-12.
  • College Board (2016), redesigned SAT: rights-only scoring. The end of the longest-running natural experiment anyone has run on pricing wrong answers.
  • AMC 10/12 scoring: 6 correct, 0 wrong, 1.5 blank, against a blind guess worth 1.2. A live, uncontroversial priced abstention, and the design this project is closer to.
  • Indian JEE, NEET and GATE; AP and GRE in earlier years. Kalai et al.'s own list of exams that price error, and their observation that all of them announce the rule in the instructions.

The models already have the signal

If a model's internal confidence were noise, this whole programme would be pointless: you cannot elicit a belief that is not there. The evidence says it is there and gets flattened. Networks stopped being calibrated out of the box around 2017. Large models can predict whether they are about to get an answer right at well above chance. They can state a probability in words and have it mean something. And the GPT-4 report contains the most-quoted plot in this literature, a base model whose stated confidence tracks its accuracy almost exactly, next to the same model after RLHF, badly overconfident.

Read together those results move the problem. It is not that models cannot tell when they are unsure. It is that nothing in training or evaluation ever asked them to say so, and at least one thing actively taught them not to.

  • Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger (2017), On calibration of modern neural networks, ICML. Accuracy improved and calibration got worse, in the same models, at the same time.
  • Kadavath et al. (2022), Language models (mostly) know what they know, arXiv:2207.05221. The self-knowledge this project assumes exists.
  • Lin, Hilton & Evans (2022), Teaching models to express their uncertainty in words, TMLR. A verbalised probability that is actually calibrated, which is the response format used here.
  • OpenAI (2023), GPT-4 technical report, calibration before and after RLHF. The clearest single picture of preference training destroying an existing signal.
  • Tian et al. (2023), Just ask for calibration, EMNLP; Xiong et al. (2024), Can LLMs express their uncertainty?, ICLR. Asking beats reading the log-probs on RLHF'd models, and overconfidence survives the asking.

The diagnosis is mainstream; the scoreboard has not moved

Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang put the argument in Nature in 2026, and it is the same argument: accuracy-maximising evaluation makes guessing optimal, so hallucination is an incentive problem before it is a modelling one. Their remedy is that benchmarks state their penalty explicitly in the rubric. This project takes that as the starting point and differs in three places. It elicits a probability rather than an answer-or-abstain decision. It pays for abstention rather than only penalising error. And it treats “does announcing the rule change what a model does” as a question to be tested rather than assumed.

Something has moved since, and it is worth being precise about how much. AA-Omniscience scores 6,000 questions at (correct − incorrect) / total, gives abstention away free, and ships inside a mainstream index rather than as a side benchmark. It reorders models: one model well down the capability tables lands near the top of it. That is the proposal working, and it retires the version of this argument that says nobody will ever price a wrong answer.

It is also the moment the standard gets set, which is the part worth caring about. Subtracting one for a wrong answer and awarding one for a right one is Kalai's threshold rule at t = 0.5. That is a legitimate rule and it is proper as a decision, but it is a decision rule: it elicits an answer-or-abstain choice and no probability, everything above the threshold scores identically, and the threshold itself is fixed at the one value that says a wrong claim costs exactly what a right one is worth. That is not a fact about language models. It is a claim about deployments, made once, silently, on behalf of everyone who reads the leaderboard. A benchmark that picks the price without saying so is making the normative call this project argues belongs to whoever operates the system.

Neither of the two things this project is about is addressed. Nothing tells a reader whether the reordering survives a different price, and the model is never told the price at all, which leaves the announcement question exactly where Kalai et al. left it. Around that, the older infrastructure still sits unused: HELM has reported calibration next to accuracy since 2022 and nobody ranks on it, and SimpleQA has graded correct, incorrect and not attempted since 2024, which is the three-way vocabulary a priced rule needs and the one this repository borrowed wholesale. The gap between metrics that are available and metrics that are decisive is the practical thing being argued about here, and it is why the argument is aimed at benchmark maintainers rather than at model trainers.

  • Kalai, Nachum, Vempala & Zhang (2026), Evaluating large language models for accuracy incentivizes hallucinations, Nature 653:1047-1051. The threshold rule this builds on, the observation that binary grading makes abstention never optimal, and a survey finding that of ten headline benchmarks, nine grade in binary and none give credit for declining.
  • Jackson, Keating, Cameron & Hill-Smith (2025), AA-Omniscience, arXiv:2511.13029. The first mainstream leaderboard to price being wrong: 6,000 questions scored (correct − incorrect) / total, abstention free, shipped inside the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. It reorders models against accuracy. It is also a single fixed price, unannounced to the model, which is the two things this project varies.
  • Wei et al. (2024), Measuring short-form factuality in large language models (SimpleQA), arXiv:2411.04368. Correct, incorrect, not attempted. The grading vocabulary already exists in a shipped benchmark.
  • Liang et al. (2022), Holistic evaluation of language models (HELM). Calibration has been a reported metric for four years, and nobody ranks on it. The cautionary case for this whole proposal.
  • Burns (2025), Measuring language model hallucinations through distributional correctness, arXiv:2510.04302. Scores the whole answer distribution rather than a thresholded decision, and separates hedging toward a wrong answer from hedging toward silence. Deliberately not a proper rule, which is precisely the choice this project declines to make.
  • Raji et al. (2021), AI and the everything in the whole wide world benchmark, NeurIPS Datasets & Benchmarks; Bowman & Dahl (2021), What will it take to fix benchmarking in NLP?, NAACL. What a benchmark is actually measuring, and what it does to the field that optimises against it.
  • Strathern (1997). Goodhart's law in the form everyone quotes: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

Putting the rule in the gradient

A sentence in the prompt is a weak instrument. If pricing error only changes behaviour while the model is being told about the pricing, the effect is compliance, and compliance decays. The stronger version makes the rule the reward, and that work is under way: refusal-aware tuning, a calibration term added to the RL reward, ternary rewards with a tunable penalty for being wrong. They converge on the finding Stage 3, the fine-tuning run, is designed to test directly. You cannot prompt your way past an objective, and a model trained under a reward that pays for saying nothing becomes a different model rather than a more obedient one.

  • Zhang et al. (2024), R-Tuning: instructing large language models to say ‘I don't know’, NAACL. Refusal-aware tuning, and the finding that the refusal generalises out of domain.
  • Damani, Puri, Slocum, Shenfeld, Choshen, Kim & Andreas (2025), Beyond binary rewards: training LMs to reason about their uncertainty, arXiv:2507.16806. The closest published relative. Reward is correctness minus a Brier term on a verbalised confidence, and only the Brier component has to be proper for the sum to elicit honestly. On Qwen2.5-7B, calibration error falls from 0.37 to 0.03 with accuracy unchanged, and the gain holds out of domain. The result that makes this cheap to adopt.
  • Mohammadi, Wang & Li (2025), Honesty over accuracy: trustworthy language models through reinforced hesitation, arXiv:2511.11500. Ternary reward, +1 / 0 / −λ, producing a frontier of models indexed by the penalty. Also the most important negative result on this site: eleven frontier models, told in the prompt that errors cost up to a hundred times a correct answer, abstain at close to zero anyway. If the Stage 1 existence check announces a rule and nothing moves, this is why.
  • Zong, Li, Long, Chang, Wu & Hadfield (2026), I-CALM, arXiv:2604.03904. The nearest thing to the Stage 1 existence check that has been published: announced incentive schemes varied in the prompt, abstention measured on TriviaQA, SimpleQA and TruthfulQA, and reported to move. It reads against the result above, and reconciling the two is now the most useful thing this project could do.
  • Kirichenko et al. (2025), AbstentionBench, arXiv:2506.09038; Wen et al. (2025), Know your limits: a survey of abstention in large language models, TACL 13:529-556. Where the abstention literature currently stands, and a map of it.

Question by question, and how big a step each one is

A gap is not automatically worth filling, and most of these are smaller than the length of the list above would suggest. The third column says what is left and then says plainly how much of a step it is, because a project that claims five contributions and has two is worse off than one that claims two.

Can a benchmark elicit an honest probability, not just a decision?Solved for human exams in 1965 and 1966, and argued over properly by decision analysts through 2013. The largest hole and the least original thinking. Every component has existed for decades; nobody has put them inside an evaluation that models are ranked on. The one open technical question is whether quadratic or logarithmic is right for a leaderboard, and Bickel argues against the choice made here.
Does any of it survive training?R-Tuning, RLCR, Reinforced Hesitation and Abstain-R1 all train abstention or calibration in, and the behaviour generalises out of domain. RLCR gets calibration essentially for free.Genuinely open, and the only causal claim available. None of them trains against a strictly proper probability-eliciting reward and then checks whether the behaviour persists with nothing announced in the prompt. That separation, reward against instruction, is the one result here that could not be assembled from existing work.

Read down the third column and the honest summary is two contributions, not five. The census is worth having because of its size and because it costs nothing to check. Stage 3, the fine-tuning run, is worth running because nobody can predict it. The middle three are a parameter sweep, a tiebreak between two published papers, and an assembly job on sixty-year-old parts, and each is worth doing mainly because it is cheap once the harness exists.

None of which is the point. The point is whether a benchmark maintainer changes anything, and against that test a small original contribution assembled into something adoptable beats a large one that stays in a paper. Most of this page is here so that the assembly can be done with the good parts rather than the nearest ones.

One correction worth making in public The Nature paper's instruction sets the penalty for a wrong answer at t / (1 − t), then lists t = 0.75 as a penalty of 2. The formula gives 3. This site and the code behind it follow the formula, so a reader comparing the two will find they disagree, and the disagreement is in the paper rather than here.
