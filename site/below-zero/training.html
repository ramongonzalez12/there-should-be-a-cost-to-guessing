What reward tuning changed

The same reinforcement-learning method trained three identical copies under three reward objectives. In the frozen final test, confidence-aware training improved proper score by 0.105, mainly through better probabilities rather than abstention.

By Ramon Gonzalez · Created 2026-08-16 10:54 ET · Last updated 2026-09-03 10:00 ETContent 2026-08-17 01:25 ET · Style 2026-09-03 10:00 ET · Organization 2026-09-03 10:00 ET

Current status The data pipeline, reward functions, base-model profiler, automatic question selector, hosted Tinker training loop, reward-scale calibration, and crossed checkpoint evaluator are complete. Two frozen training seeds have now run all three reward arms for one pass over the same 500 questions. The preregistered final test then generated 6,400 responses on 200 untouched items. Confidence-aware training beat accuracy-only training by 0.105 proper-score units, with a 95% interval from 0.036 to 0.175. Accuracy changed by -2.0 points, inside the prespecified safeguard. The added assertion cost produced no stable incremental benefit. Two further base model families have since replicated the confidence-aware training effect, GPT-OSS 20B and Nemotron 3 Nano 30B (an early Nemotron profiling attempt failed the untouched-base format gate; a syntax warm-up then cleared it and training completed). Across all three trained families, 29 of 33 matched pairs show the confidence-calibrated score improving.

The abstention interpretation All three tuned checkpoints retained 100% coverage. The confidence objective changed how uncertainty was reported without teaching the model to withhold low-confidence views. That is the useful behavior for this setting: share the best answer with calibrated confidence, and reserve literal abstention for rare cases below an explicit action threshold.

Conclusion The cross-family result is promising but not established. Seed 0 shows that the confidence reward can create meaningful differentiation in Nemotron after a shared syntax lesson. Seed 1 shows that the lesson was not robust enough to support the planned two-seed causal claim. Replacing the failed seed, relaxing the gate, or opening the final set would turn a technical failure into a post-result design change, so paid work stopped.

Fine-tuning, in one sentence

A pretrained model already knows how to answer questions. Fine-tuning gives it repeated practice under a particular definition of a good answer, then makes small adjustments so responses that earned more reward become more likely next time.

We are not trying to teach the model medicine, science, or law. The questions come from subjects it already encountered during general training. We first want a better reported belief: which answer is best and how likely it is to be correct. A downstream decision policy can then use the same probability to decide whether an assertion is worth making under a particular cost.

The whole experiment: tune, test, then score

There are not three different fine-tuning technologies. We use the same off-the-shelf RL and LoRA process three times. Only the reward objective changes. After training ends, every resulting checkpoint goes through every evaluation instruction. Every saved response is then scored under every formula.

What happens during one training step

Step 1AskGive the model one multiple-choice question with an explicit “I don't know” option.
Step 2SampleGenerate eight independent responses to the same question, each with an answer and a stated probability.
Step 3ScoreApply the arm's reward rule automatically. No human judges which response sounds better.
Step 4UpdateNudge a small adapter toward the responses that scored better than the other responses in that group.

Repeat across the frozen training questions.

The comparison within each group is why the candidate questions matter. If all eight responses are correct, or all eight are wrong, an accuracy reward gives them the same score and there is no learning signal. We first profile the unchanged base model, then automatically retain questions where its outcomes are mixed. The same final questions go to every arm.

One response, all the way from reward to weights

Suppose two of eight responses in the accuracy arm are correct. Their rewards are 1 and the other six rewards are 0, so the group average is 0.25. Each correct response gets a relative signal of +0.75; each incorrect response gets −0.25. The confidence arms use continuous rewards, but the conversion is the same:

relative signal = response reward − average reward for that question
positive signal → make this completion more likely
negative signal → make this completion less likely
  1. The sampler records what the current model did. Along with the answer, it saves the token sequence and the probability the current policy assigned to each sampled token.
  2. The reward becomes a sequence-level learning signal. Prompt tokens receive zero. Every token in the short two-line completion receives that response's relative signal.
  3. Backpropagation measures responsibility. Tinker computes how a tiny change to each trainable LoRA weight would have changed the likelihood of those sampled tokens. This is a gradient: a direction and magnitude, not an edited answer stored in the model.
  4. Adam takes a small step. The optimizer combines the current gradient with recent gradient history, scales it by the learning rate, and updates only the adapter weights. Weights associated with positive-signal token patterns move up; negative ones move down.
  5. The next batch uses the updated adapter. New responses are sampled from the new policy, scored again, and the loop repeats. Generalization occurs only if thousands of these local nudges add up to a behavior that also appears on unseen questions.

What actually changes in the model?

The base model has billions of learned weights. We leave those weights frozen and attach a much smaller set of trainable weights using LoRA. Those adapter weights learn the new behavior. This makes the run cheaper, lets every arm start from the exact same base, and leaves us with a small checkpoint that can be evaluated or removed independently.

Think of the base model as an experienced analyst and the adapter as a new compensation policy. The analyst's underlying education does not change during this short experiment. What changes is which available behavior gets reinforced.

Are we using an off-the-shelf method?

Mostly. The GPU machinery is standard rather than a new learning algorithm. The hosted loop follows Thinking Machines' minimal group-relative RL recipe: LoRA adapters, groups of sampled completions, group-centered advantages, an importance-sampling policy-gradient loss, backpropagation, and Adam. Tinker runs the distributed GPU operations and checkpointing. We supply the prompts, deterministic rewards, strict parser, question selection, paired arms, health gates, and evaluation.

There is no learned reward model, human preference labeling, or bespoke neural-network architecture. The custom part is the experiment: what counts as reward and how we keep that one difference identifiable.

Key design questions and our current decisions

  • Reward shape versus update size. Subtracting the group mean removes reward level, but not reward spread. The selector now uses the unchanged-base profile to calculate one fixed scale factor per arm, so the arms start with the same root mean square relative signal. A single positive multiplier preserves each rule's incentives and its differences across questions; normalizing every question separately would not. The manifest saves the raw and scaled spreads, and training stops if the calibration belongs to different data or a different assertion cost. A later raw-scale run is a declared sensitivity, not the primary comparison.
  • How tightly to constrain policy drift. The initial loop uses the standard importance-sampling loss because every rollout is nearly on-policy and receives one update. We should record divergence from the base model, entropy, and update norms. If a small pilot shows unstable ratios, switch all arms together to a clipped PPO or CISPO loss, or add the same reference-policy penalty to every arm.
  • How much learning capacity to allow. LoRA rank, learning rate, group size, and number of steps jointly determine how far the policy can move. Fix them across arms and use the smallest settings that create a measurable change on a separate validation slice without degrading general accuracy. The held-out test cannot choose these settings.
  • Whether sequence-level credit is adequate. One final reward is applied to every completion token, so the optimizer is not told whether the answer letter or probability caused the score. The exact two-line output keeps that ambiguity small. We should not add free-form reasoning to the training response unless we also redesign credit assignment.
  • How to choose a checkpoint. The primary comparison should use the final checkpoint at a step count fixed in advance. Selecting each arm's best checkpoint on the held-out test would give three chances to overfit. Intermediate checkpoints can guide a later run only on a separate validation slice.

How to make the reward differences easier to see

There are two different ways to seek a larger effect. We can make sure the reward arms actually send different learning signals to the optimizer, or we can train harder on whatever signal already exists. The first is an identification requirement. The second is an effect-size choice. More steps, a higher learning rate, or a larger adapter cannot rescue reward arms whose post-centering signals are the same.

The important mathematical trap The optimizer never receives the raw reward by itself. It receives each response's reward minus the average reward in that eight-response group. Any reward term that is the same for all eight responses disappears. That means a larger assertion cost has no training effect in a group where every response still asserts an answer.

One tuning method, three reward objectives

Every arm starts from the same base model and uses the same prompts, training questions, deterministic order, and sampling settings. Only the reward changes. Stochastic completions are independently generated and saved; setting one provider seed for an eight-sample call made all eight outputs identical and was rejected by the health gate. Each finished arm is a saved checkpoint: the unchanged base weights plus that arm's small LoRALow-rank adaptation. A small set of extra weights trained alongside a frozen base model, cheap to train and to store. When these results say the weights changed, it is the adapter that changed, not the base. adapter.

What the accuracy-only RL baseline means It is not the default form of all fine-tuning. It is the conventional comparator for reinforcement learning on questions with verifiable answers, and it mirrors the usual benchmark objective: correct earns 1; wrong or abstaining earns 0. The optimizer makes higher-reward completion patterns more likely. The model still reports a probability, but that number has no effect on this arm's reward. This short run supplies no explanatory lessons; using untouched held-out questions tests whether it learned a general policy rather than memorizing training answers.

A concrete example

Suppose the model can internally distinguish a question where it is 60% likely to be right from one where it is only 20% likely to be right. The reward rules imply different best behavior:

model's beliefaccuracy rewardconfidence rewardconfidence + cost
60% likely correctAnswerAnswer; report 60%Answer; report 60%
20% likely correctAnswerAnswer; report 20%Abstain

For the confidence-plus-cost rule, the break-even confidence is √0.09 = 30%. Below that point, an honest answer has a negative expected score and explicit abstention scores zero.

This is the causal question. Accuracy training may teach “take the shot.” Confidence training may teach “say how difficult the shot is.” Adding the assertion cost may teach “do not take the shot when the odds are poor.”

Scoring is a third axis, but it does not require more model calls

Every saved response contains an answer, a probability, and the explicit abstention choice. We can therefore score that one response afterwards under accuracy, confidence, and confidence-plus-cost. The full analysis is training reward × evaluation prompt × scoring lens. Only the first two axes require generation; applying all three scoring rules afterwards is deterministic and free.

The reward-specific scores are useful, but they should not be the primary verdict. A model trained on one objective is expected to look best under that same objective. The common endpoints are harder to game: overall accuracy, coverage, incorrect assertions, calibration, and the full error-versus-coverage curve.

The primary test and the secondary diagnostics

  • Primary: persistence with no reward announced. Compare all three adapters and the base model on identical untouched questions. This is the clean test of what training changed.
  • Primary endpoints: belief quality and capability. Compare quadratic proper score, Brier score, calibration gap, confidence AUROC, overall accuracy, and format failure. A model should report probabilities that correspond to correctness while preserving its ability to answer.
  • Headline contrast. Compare confidence-aware training with accuracy-only training under the neutral prompt. A practical win requires a reproducible proper-score or calibration improvement without a material loss of overall accuracy.
  • Derived action policies. Apply multiple confidence thresholds to every checkpoint's saved probabilities and report the resulting risk-coverage curves. These policies do not require retraining or more generation calls.
  • Secondary: prompt-by-training interactions. The three announced-rule rows show whether the learned policy remains flexible. A cost-trained adapter that abstains under every instruction may be rigid; one that changes appropriately with the announced objective has learned a more configurable policy.
  • Paired uncertainty. Compute item-level differences because every checkpoint sees the same questions, report uncertainty by held-out source, and require a promising first-seed effect to repeat in a second complete training seed before opening the final test.
  • No test-driven checkpoint choice. Freeze the held-out items and primary contrasts before training. The test set is opened once for the final comparison; it is not a dashboard for choosing learning rates or stopping steps.
  • One declared success contrast. The matrix creates many legitimate comparisons, but not many chances to declare victory. Before training, name the primary adapter contrast, common endpoint, and smallest effect worth replicating. Treat the rest as diagnostics with uncertainty, not as alternate routes to a positive result.

What counted as meaningful differentiation?

A lower training loss did not count, and neither did a visible difference in training responses. The reward directly creates both. The evidence had to appear on questions the adapters never trained on. The final test met the first, second, and replication criteria below without relying on abstention.

  • Better probability quality. The confidence-aware arm should improve the proper score and reduce probability error relative to accuracy-only training.
  • Better calibration and ranking. A stated 70% should be right about 70% of the time, and higher probabilities should reliably distinguish correct answers from incorrect ones.
  • Useful derived policies. Applying a downstream confidence threshold should reduce error as coverage falls. Rare abstention is acceptable when few beliefs fall below the chosen threshold.
  • Cost-policy consistency. A cost-trained model is a mechanism test: it should abstain when its own reported probability is below the announced break-even threshold and answer when above it. Raw abstention frequency is not a primary endpoint.
  • Replication across seeds. One seed is an inexpensive existence test. The development effect had to survive a second complete paired seed before it could justify a final test. Additional seeds and model families would be needed for a broad generalization claim.

What requires human attention?

Operational record: what is automatic and what needs a decision

Model-response inspection is not part of the operating plan. Parsing, answer grading, reward calculation, contamination checks, source quotas, answer-letter balance, question selection, rollout-health checks, spend bounds, and artifact saving are deterministic and automatic. A failed gate stops the run rather than asking someone to clean up rows by hand.

The human decisions are substantive: authorize paid execution, choose the assertion cost, and decide whether the held-out effect is large enough to justify replication. Those are design and spending choices, not labeling work.
