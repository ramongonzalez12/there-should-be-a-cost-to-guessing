What is new here, section by section

Each section of the argument, next to the closest published research: what that work established, what it did not test, and what this project adds.

By Ramon Gonzalez · Created 2026-08-25 00:23 ET · Last updated 2026-09-03 10:00 ETContent 2026-08-25 16:57 ET · Style 2026-08-25 01:13 ET · Organization 2026-09-03 10:00 ET

This page follows the write-up section by section. For each claim it collects the nearest primary research, states what that work already establishes, and then says what this project tests that the prior work did not. What this builds on lists the older foundations by topic; this page is organized by the argument instead.

How to read the labels. Direct Same question and a similar test. Close Same question or method, but a materially different test. Adjacent Supports the idea without testing this comparison. ●○○ Established ground: prior work answers the same question; this project adds scale or precision. ●●○ Sharper contribution: the question is live in prior work; this project's version is tighter or better formed. ●●● New comparison: no direct precedent for the test itself was found in this review. Novelty claims are limited to the primary research reviewed through August 24, 2026. “Not tested there” means none of the cited work ran that comparison, not that no one ever has.
How much is new, at a glance.
●○○ Always answering: the diagnosis is established; the census scale is the addition.
●●○ Scoring rules: priced grading is already shipping; the strictly proper, probability-eliciting version is the proposal here.
●●○ Prompting: well-studied question; the matched share-of-gap estimate is the addition.
●●○ Training: direct precedents exist; the same-rule prompt-versus-training contrast and the directional transfer matrix are the additions.
●●● Behavior chain: no direct matched test of the chain was found; the mostly negative result is the finding.
○○○ Next tests: open ground; proposed here, not yet run.

Models answer everything and rarely doubt themselves

●○○ Established ground. The incentive diagnosis is prior work's; the census of published behavior at scale is the addition.

The write-up opens with a behavioral claim: in published benchmark runs, models almost never decline to answer, and their stated confidence runs far ahead of their accuracy. The diagnosis behind that behavior is established. The census that measures how completely it holds in the wild is the newer part.

Where the always-answering claim stands
Nearest prior work
LLM research, 2025 to 2026. Direct Kalai et al. (2025) show formally that binary accuracy grading rewards guessing over admitting uncertainty. Close AbstentionBench (2025) finds abstention unreliable across 20 models and 20 purpose-built datasets, and that reasoning fine-tuning makes it 24% worse on average. Close ConfidenceBench (2026) elicits probabilities from 15 frontier models on 200 questions across four categories, repeated three times. Accuracy and confidence-calibration rankings diverge substantially.
What it did not test
How often models actually decline in the benchmark runs that laboratories publish. AbstentionBench asks specially built questions designed to need abstention; it does not count behavior on ordinary benchmark items, which is where the incentive to guess actually operates.
What this project adds
A census of published behavior, followed by new confidence-eliciting tests. Across 1,089,024 graded responses from 104 models on 36 benchmarks, 0.69% ever decline and 0.25% state a probability. Alongside it, an audit of 45 benchmark scoring records shows how rarely a wrong answer scores below silence. We then go beyond stored Humanity's Last Exam answers by explicitly eliciting confidence on six other benchmarks. Across 27 model-benchmark comparisons, 10 models, seven benchmarks, and 5,376 answers, every comparison was overconfident: 55% accuracy versus 87% confidence.

The counts themselves are on the findings page and in the table brief.

A penalty for guessing, and which one

●●○ Sharper proposal. The rule is classical and priced grading is already shipping; the strictly proper, probability-eliciting version for LLM benchmarks is the proposal here.

The second section proposes scoring wrong answers below silence with the quadratic rule 2p − p². Proper scoring itself is not new: the theory is more than seventy years old, and educational testing applied guessing penalties for decades. The application is where the ground is open. Every priced rule now in circulation for language models is a decision rule (answer or abstain, at some penalty); none elicits the probability itself, and the norm is being set now.

Where the scoring proposal stands
Nearest prior work
Outside LLMs, 1950 to 2007. Direct Brier, Savage, and Gneiting and Raftery built the theory of strictly proper scoring rules under which honest probability reporting is the best strategy. Close Human test scoring in the 1960s applied those rules, and Chow (1970) formalized declining to classify when confidence is too low. LLM benchmarks, 2025. Direct Kalai et al. call for explicit confidence targets in benchmark instructions. Close AA-Omniscience prices wrong answers across 6,000 questions, 42 topics, and six domains.
What it did not test
Kalai et al.'s remedy is a threshold rule: proper as a decision, but it elicits an answer-or-abstain choice and no probability, and the price must be fixed before generation. AA-Omniscience hard-codes that price with a linear rule, abstention scoring zero and the price never announced to the model, a form that cannot elicit a probability at all. Neither, nor the ternary-reward variants, obtains the number that would let a reader re-price wrongness after the fact.
What this project adds
The strictly proper version, and the conditions for it to matter. Because the rule elicits the full probability, the deployment price becomes a transparent, adjustable parameter applied after generation rather than a constant baked silently into the leaderboard. Five candidate rules implemented with numerical properness checks, plus a structural check across 69 published GPQA runs: without an explicit I DON'T KNOW option, any penalty is an affine rescaling of accuracy and cannot change a single ranking.

The full lineage, from weather forecasting through formula scoring to selective classification, is on What this builds on.

Telling the model about the rule barely works

●●○ Sharper estimate. Announced-payoff prompting has direct precedents; the matched share-of-gap magnitude is the addition.

The write-up's prompting section reports that explaining the scoring formula changes behavior only a little. Prompting for confidence has a real literature, and one strand comes close to this exact experiment. None of it, including the Kalai et al. proposal that benchmarks announce confidence targets, measures how much of an existing overconfidence gap an announced rule closes.

Where the prompting result stands
Nearest prior work
LLM research, 2023 to 2026. Direct Tian et al. (2023) test ChatGPT, GPT-4, and Claude on three benchmarks; verbalized confidence often cuts calibration error by 50% relative to token probabilities. Direct Xiong et al. (2023 to 2024) test five LLMs across five dataset types and find no elicitation method works consistently. Direct I-CALM (2026) announces answer-or-abstain payoffs and improves selective answering. Direct Honesty over Accuracy (2025) finds severe prompt penalties alone produce little abstention.
What it did not test
Kalai et al. propose announcing targets but run no experiment on whether the announcement works. The elicitation papers measure calibration under different phrasings; they do not announce a strictly proper scoring contract and then quantify the share of a measured gap it closes.
What this project adds
A matched magnitude estimate, and it is small. On identical frozen questions across five current models and three hard benchmarks, explaining the scoring formula without an answering threshold closed 16% of the confidence gap and left 84% in place. The comparison contains 4,536 matched responses. It isolates the formula explanation from the separate threshold question.

The experiment is in the table brief.

Training on the rule works, and it transfers

●●○ Sharper comparison. Every component has a direct precedent; the same-rule prompt-versus-training contrast and the directional subject matrix are the additions.

The training section makes the stronger claim: fine-tuning on the penalized score calibrates models quickly, and the calibration carries into subjects the training never touched. Calibration-aware training has direct precedents. The comparison the write-up leans on, prompting against training under one rule, is the part this review did not find elsewhere.

Where the training result stands
Nearest prior work
LLM research, 2023 to 2026. Direct RLCR (2025 to 2026) adds a Brier term to correctness reward and improves calibration in and out of domain without losing accuracy. Direct Steyvers et al. (2025 to 2026) fine-tune two models on general knowledge, mathematics, and trivia, then test unseen medical, legal, and truthfulness benchmarks. Gains transfer, but less than within domain. Direct R-Tuning (2023) shows refusal training can become an out-of-domain meta-skill. Direct Tan et al. (2026) warn that badly designed confidence rewards can be gamed.
What it did not test
None of these compares an announced rule against training on that same rule inside one harness on frozen questions, so none can say how much of the effect requires changing the weights. And none reports a directional subject-by-subject transfer matrix from independently trained pairs.
What this project adds
The prompt-versus-training contrast, and a transfer matrix. Explaining the same strictly proper formula closed 16% of the confidence gap; training on it closed 81%. Matched training arms differ only in reward: same starting checkpoint, same items, same seed. Across seven independent Qwen pairs, the outside-subject reduction in high-confidence errors was 98% as large as the same-subject reduction, held on GPQA, which supplied no training data, and the pooled pattern repeated in GPT-OSS.
What it still cannot show
Final outside-subject calibration stayed worse than same-subject calibration; the training transferred the size of the correction, not equal mastery. The directional matrix exists in one model family so far.

The matched tables are in the table brief, with seeds and health gates on the replication page.

Calibration improves prudence, not alignment in general

●●● New comparison. No direct matched test of the calibration-to-behavior chain was found in this review.

The closing section is the honest one: calibrated models gate their own risky answers far better, and that is where the good news stops. The idea that uncertainty makes agents safer has a long theoretical pedigree. The matched experiment that checks each link of that chain after calibration training is what this review could not find.

Where the behavior chain stands
Nearest prior work
Outside LLMs, 2016. Adjacent The Off-Switch Game and CIRL show formally that objective uncertainty can make agents accept correction. LLM systems and alignment, 2023 to 2025. Close KnowNo (2023) converts calibrated uncertainty into asking for help across simulated and real robot tasks, at runtime and without fine-tuning. Close Kumaran et al. (2025) measure how commitment and criticism distort confidence and evidence updating. Adjacent Pluralistic alignment (2024) defines three forms of pluralism and three benchmark families for the many-values problem.
What it did not test
Whether training a model to be calibrated about facts changes any downstream behavior: risky decisions, evidence updating, goal revision, or taking a prohibited shortcut. The theory papers assume the link; the measurement papers observe single pieces without intervening on calibration.
What this project adds
A matched test of several links at once, mostly negative. Confidence training removed 97% of overconfident wrong actions in a structured decision test. It did not improve evidence updating or goal revision, and it removed only 2% of prohibited hidden shortcuts. A one-line plural-ends reminder did far more for shortcuts, cutting them from 6.8% to 1.0% across all 16 tested checkpoints.
What it still cannot show
The shortcut scenarios are short and transparent. They measure rule-following under mild temptation, not scheming, and SHADE-Arena remains the stronger model of the behavior that matters.

The chain results are in the table brief.

The decisive test exists and has not been run

○○○ Open ground. No precedent and no result either; this is where the next increment of novelty is available.

The write-up closes by arguing that scoring has more than one axis. The literature already supplies the environments where that claim gets its real test; what is missing is running matched calibration-trained checkpoints inside them.

Where the next step stands
Nearest prior work
Direct SHADE-Arena pairs long benign tasks with hidden sabotage objectives under monitoring. Direct AgentMisalignment elicits shutdown resistance, sandbagging, and power seeking. Direct MACHIAVELLI exposes reward-versus-ethics tradeoffs across 134 games. Direct AgentHarm tests harmful compliance alongside retained competence.
What it did not test
Every one of these compares models, prompts, or personas. None compares training objectives: the same checkpoint trained with accuracy-only reward against the same checkpoint trained with a proper confidence reward, dropped into the same hidden-opportunity environment.
What this project should add next
The missing combination. Run the matched accuracy-trained and confidence-trained pairs in these environments, keep benign controls so lower misconduct cannot be bought with general refusal, and treat the independently trained pair as the replication unit. That experiment would test the alignment claim directly. Enlarging the current shortcut battery would not.

The candidate designs are on the open questions page and at the end of the table brief.
