What survived a second training run

Three reward objectives, each trained twice from a different random seed and measured against the untuned model it started from. The directions replicated. The magnitudes did not. This page states which is which, and separates what a model was trained on from what its answers were scored with, because conflating those two is how a result like this gets misread.

By Ramon Gonzalez · Created 2026-08-20 01:05 ET · Last updated 2026-09-03 10:00 ETContent 2026-08-17 01:24 ET · Style 2026-09-03 10:00 ET · Organization 2026-09-03 10:00 ET

A scoring rule can play three different roles

The same rule, say the quadratic score, can be the thing a model is told about, the thing it is trained on, or the thing its answers are scored with afterwards. Those are three separate questions with three separate answers, and most confusion about work like this comes from reading a result about one of them as a result about another.

where a rule appearsthe question that askson this page
Announced in the promptDoes telling a model how it will be scored change what it does?Held fixed. Nothing was announced. Every answer here was produced under a neutral prompt.
Used as the training rewardDoes training against the rule change the policy itself?Varies. This is the experiment: three rewards, two runs each.
Applied to stored answers afterwardsHow does the resulting policy score under any rule, including ones it never optimised?Varies, and costs nothing. Stored responses are rescored offline under four rules.

Because the third role is applied after generation to stored responses, it is free and it is unlimited: the same answers can be scored under any number of rules without running the model again. This page uses two of them, and only two. Everything below reports plain accuracy, which is what benchmarks do today, and the quadratic score, which is what this project argues they should do instead. Nothing here is managed, decided or stopped against any other rule, so putting more columns on the page would ask the reader to price numbers nobody acts on.

What each version was trained on

Three rewards were trained, twice each. The difference between the first two is exactly one thing: whether the probability the model states changes what it earns.

Each was trained twice, at five doses: 125, 250, 300, 375 and 500 steps. Same frozen training pool, same 100 held-out questions, same neutral prompt.

What differs between the two runs is narrower than "the random seed", and saying so matters for how wide the gaps below should be read. The two runs saw the same questions in the same order and started from the same weights. The provider's sampler draws the training rollouts without a fixed seed, deliberately: pinning one made all eight samples in a group come back identical, which erases the group-relative comparison the training method runs on. So the sampling stream is the only thing that diverged.

That makes these two runs a lower bound on how much a retrained model moves. The two variance sources usually largest in this kind of training, which questions arrive in which order and how the adapter is initialised, are held at zero here. A reader should treat the gaps below as the least a rerun would produce, not the most. It also means neither run can be reproduced exactly, only repeated.

What a score change is measured with

Two measures, reported for every version at every dose. The distance between them is the argument: they agree about the confidence-aware versions and disagree about the accuracy-only one, and that disagreement is the whole result.

Plain accuracy is what benchmarks already report. A right answer scores 1, and a wrong answer and a silence both score 0. The quadratic score is the proposed replacement. It is strictly proper, which is a technical property with a plain meaning: a model gets its best expected score by reporting the probability it actually holds, so no confidence it can claim beats telling the truth.

Both runs measured this independently and they agree. Run 1 re-evaluated its base at three stages and spanned 0.014 on the quadratic score; run 2 did it at five and spanned 0.021. These are draws of the same quantity, because the untuned base is one fixed model answering one fixed set of questions, so all eight pool into a single floor: a standard deviation of 0.007 and a full range of 0.021. The floor is measured, not assumed.

It is worth being exact about what this floor is, because it is not sampling randomness. Generation is greedy, at temperature zero, with a fixed seed, so these eight passes should have been identical. They differ because floating-point addition is not associative and the work is batched differently each time, which flips the answer on items where the top two options are nearly tied. The signature is visible in the responses: about 90 of the 100 items are identical every single time, and two items carry roughly 85% of the spread, one of them a single multiple-choice letter flipping from A to C. So this is a handful of coin flips on knife-edge questions rather than a smooth bell curve, and a ± band is a convenient summary of something lumpier than that notation suggests.

Practically: a change of 0.02 in the quadratic column is roughly the size of the gap between measuring one unchanged model twice. Any number smaller than its measure's band should be read as "no detectable change", not as a small effect. The band is drawn on the charts further down and greyed in a table where it applies. Every averaged cell in the next section clears its band; several individual doses do not, which is a distinction a single averaged number would hide.

Read down a column, not across a row. The two measures have different natural scales, so the accuracy and quadratic numbers in one row are not comparable to each other in size, only in sign.

The top row-pair is the result, and it is the only place the two columns disagree. Training on accuracy alone raises plain accuracy and lowers the quadratic score, in both runs. A benchmark reporting only the first column would record that trade as an unambiguous improvement, and would be measuring a model that got worse at knowing what it knows.

Both confidence-aware row-pairs are positive in both columns in both runs. Pricing confidence did not cost accuracy in either trajectory, which is the claim worth carrying forward: the trade the first row shows is a property of the accuracy-only reward, not a price every version pays.

What replicated and what did not

The question a second run exists to answer is whether the direction holds, so that is what this counts: for each version, under each measure, how many of the five doses moved the same way in both runs.

Now the same measurements drawn, so the gap between the runs is visible rather than summarised. The shaded band is the repeat-measurement floor from above.

Two things are true at once here, and the page would be misleading if it printed only the first. The directions replicated. Accuracy-only training lowered the quadratic score in ten out of ten measurements across two runs; the confidence-aware versions raised it in eighteen of twenty, the exceptions being the assertion-cost version at 500 steps in run 1 and at 375 steps in run 2, both within a hair of zero. The magnitudes did not. Nine of the ten confidence-aware measurements came back smaller in run 2, and on plain accuracy fourteen of fifteen did. That is a systematic shift, not scatter, and far too large to be explained by the 0.008 difference between the two runs' bases.

Run 1 was the more favourable trajectory. Any single number quoted from it, such as the +0.155 that the cost-0.09 version reached at 125 steps, is a property of that trajectory: the same recipe with a different seed returned +0.029. Where an earlier page quoted such figures, read them as one draw.

The ordering within the confidence-aware group did not survive either, and the earlier page said in advance that it should not be quoted. The assertion-cost version produced the single largest number in either run, +0.155 at 125 steps, and led at the two lowest doses of run 1. In run 2 it led at one dose out of five. The two versions are close enough that a rerun reorders them, so they should not be reported as distinguishable, and the assertion cost should not be presented as buying anything on this evidence.

The part that replicated most cleanly

Underneath the score changes is a behavioural one, and it is the most consistent result in either run.

Training on accuracy alone drove abstention to exactly zero in both runs at all five doses, ten out of ten measurements, from a base that declined to answer about one question in ten. It also pushed stated confidence up, from 0.88 to above 0.91 everywhere. The model stopped saying it did not know, and became more certain while doing it.

That is the finding this project began from, reproduced causally: a census of published benchmark responses found 0.68% ever say they don't know, and here is a reward that produces exactly that, from a model that started out willing to. It does not depend on which scoring rule you grade with, which is why it is the claim to carry rather than any of the score deltas above.

The same thing shows up as a number, without needing a scoring rule at all. The calibration gap is how confident a version says it is, minus how often that confidence turns out to be right. Positive means overconfident. It is a subtraction rather than a rule, so nobody has to accept the quadratic score to read it.

Training on accuracy alone made the model more overconfident than it started, at every dose in both runs. The base overstates itself by 0.26, and the two runs agree on that to within 0.006. Accuracy-only training pushes the gap to between 0.34 and 0.38, ten measurements out of ten. Every confidence-aware measurement moves the other way, also ten out of ten. The assertion-cost version is the noisy one again, better than the base at nine of its ten.

This is the cleanest statement of the result available, because it needs no argument about scoring. A benchmark that reports accuracy would show the accuracy-only version improving. It is, at the same time, further from knowing what it knows than the untuned model it started as.

The confidence-aware versions do not restore abstention to base levels either. They keep it near zero while pulling stated confidence down to between 0.69 and 0.87, which is the calibration effect showing up as hedged answers rather than as silence. One exception appears in both runs and is unexplained: at 375 steps the assertion-cost version abstains far more than at any neighbouring dose, 9% in run 1 and 14% in run 2 against roughly 1% elsewhere. It is the only dose in the grid where a tuned version declines to answer more often than the untuned model.

What this still cannot tell you

Two runs. Two is enough to show that a magnitude moved between them and not enough to say what its distribution is, so nothing here is an interval. The right reading of a two-run agreement is "the direction is probably real", not "the effect is this big". And as the versions section says, these two runs differ only in the sampling stream, so even that is the narrowest version of the question.

The step counts are not a matched dose across versions. Training only learns from a batch when the eight sampled answers to a question disagree with each other; when all eight score the same there is nothing to compare, and the batch is skipped. Under the accuracy-only reward, where a response can only score 1 or 0, that happens almost every time: 8% of its batches produce a learning signal on average, against 66% for the confidence-aware versions, which can also separate answers by the probability attached to them. Counted over the full 500 steps, accuracy-only absorbs about 150 productive batches against about 1,300, a factor of nearly nine. The pipeline already knows this and admits each version against its own floor for how many batches must have been productive. Read the doses as elapsed training, not as equal treatment.

That asymmetry is not a flaw in the setup so much as a property of the reward being tested. A rule that scores only right-or-wrong has less to say about eight answers than one that also reads their stated confidence, and having less to say is part of what is being argued about it. But it does mean a dose-for-dose comparison flatters neither side cleanly, and no claim here should rest on the two versions having been trained equally hard.
