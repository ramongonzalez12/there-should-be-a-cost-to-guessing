Below Zero

Benchmarks should ask a model how sure it is, and say what a confident mistake costs. Almost none of them do either.

Across 1,089,024 published model responses, 0.69% decline to answer. Asked for a probability with every answer, ten current models were wrong at 90%+ stated confidence on 12 of every 100 questions, and only training on a score that prices that error changed it.

Summary

How a test is scored matters. Almost every AI benchmark pays one point for a correct answer and nothing for a wrong one or for silence, so a model that is unsure has no reason to say so. This site measures how completely that is true, proposes a score that prices confident error, and tests what changes a model's calibration: telling it the rule, telling it to hedge, or training it on the score.

  • Models almost never decline. Across 1,089,024 published responses from 104 models on 36 benchmarks, 0.69% contain any admission of not knowing.
  • Confidence does not track difficulty. Ten current models, asked for a probability with every answer on the same frozen questions, ran about 12 points more confident than accurate, 22 points on the three hardest benchmarks, and were wrong at 90%+ stated confidence on 12 of every 100 questions.
  • Only training on the score moves the score. Explaining the rule in the prompt changes nothing measurable. Telling the model to be less confident closes the gap but overshoots and leaves the proper score flat. Fine-tuning on the score cut the gap from +26 to +16 points across ten runs, raised the score from 32 to 38, and cut near-certain wrong answers from 12 to 2 per 100.

The full account, with every number asserted against the committed data, is the essay. This page is the short version and the map.

What moves calibration, and by how much

Three interventions were compared on the same three outcomes: the overconfidence gap (mean stated confidence minus accuracy), a proper score (accuracy minus Brier loss, on a 0-to-100 scale), and the share of questions answered wrongly at 90%+ confidence. The ordering is the finding.

  • Explaining the scoring rule: nothing. Randomized against a plain baseline on ten frontier models and eight benchmarks, every outcome moved by less than a point, inside its interval.
  • Telling the model to hedge: the number, not the knowledge. A one-sentence warning about overconfidence cut the gap by 17 points and near-certain errors by 10 per 100, but the proper score stayed flat, because a blunt confidence cut pays back on hard questions exactly what it costs on easy ones.
  • Training on the score: the knowledge. Across ten Qwen3 8B runs the post-warm-up gap fell from +26 to +16, the score rose from 32 to 38, and near-certain errors fell from 12 to 2 per 100; every one of the ten runs improved. Matched accuracy-only training moved the other way on that track (gap +33). The improvement carried to held-out subjects consistently for Qwen3 8B and unevenly for GPT-OSS 20B, and on a decision battery it nearly eliminated wrong actions taken at 90%+ confidence in both families without moving broader alignment measures.

On the same 800 questions and the same two open-weight checkpoints, in commensurate units: explaining the rule costs about 4 points of score, hedging buys about 4, and training on the score buys about 11. Differences under about two points are inside this design's run-to-run noise.

Announcing a rule can change whether a model answers, not how well it knows

Two results on this site look like they disagree and do not. In the free-form existence check of August 15 (50 SimpleQA questions, six models), announcing the score together with a 30% abstention threshold raised explicit "I don't know" answers by 34 points for two of the six models and by 14 for a third, with matching drops in error. On the eight multiple-choice benchmarks of the main study, the same rule leaves confidence, accuracy, and the proper score where they were.

Both are true. A rule can move the decision to answer a hard open question; it does not move the calibration of the answers a model gives, because nothing in a prompt tells the model which of its answers are the wrong ones. That is what training on the score does, and why it is the intervention that moves the score.

On September 2, 2026 a review of the grading code found that two benchmarks in the main study had been graded against a stand-in item with no answer choices, so every answer of "A" was recorded as an abstention and the real abstention letter as a wrong answer; a second defect meant MMLU-Pro abstentions could never count. Every retained run was re-parsed and re-graded from its stored raw text, no model was re-queried, and the tables on this site and in the essay carry the corrected numbers. The headline gap moved from +13 to +12 and the training result did not move at all.

How often models decline

The census scans every per-question model output in the public EvalEval datastore for roughly eighteen ways of saying "I don't know", anywhere in the generation. Decline language clusters where the environment can show the model that it failed; on closed-form question answering it is nearly absent.

About me & my motivation

I spent years in quantitative finance before moving into healthcare analytics, and in both the same lesson kept surfacing: what you choose to measure ends up being what you get, whatever you intended. A metric with no penalty for overconfidence produces confident people.

I don't think AI benchmarks are badly designed by carelessness. I think a leaderboard needs a single number to sort on, and the easiest single number to justify is the fraction correct, which quietly sets the price of being wrong to zero. That price should be visible and arguable, not invisible and fixed.
