There should be a penalty for guessing

How accuracy-only benchmarks reward confident guesses, how to score honest confidence, and what changed when models were prompted and trained on that rule.

By Ramon Gonzalez · Created 2026-08-20 01:09 ET · Last updated 2026-09-03 10:00 ETContent 2026-08-24 23:48 ET · Style 2026-08-25 01:13 ET · Organization 2026-09-03 10:00 ET

This brief makes one recommendation: benchmarks should ask models how likely their answers are to be correct, then score those probabilities. If a benchmark also wants models to decline low-confidence questions, it should provide an explicit “I don't know” option.

The findings

  • Models almost always answer. They declined only 0.45% of closed-form questions in more than one million published responses.
  • Current scoring rewards that behavior. Nearly every benchmark we reviewed gives a wrong answer and no answer the same score.
  • Confidence can be changed. In the expanded prompt test, the intended rule reduced the median absolute confidence gap by 3.3 points across five models from four developers. Training on the rule produced a larger gain in one model family.
  • Calibration improved prudence much more than alignment behavior. Pooled training cut high-confidence errors by 13 to 51 points across four subjects. Hidden unauthorized shortcuts fell by only 1 to 3 points at the median and still occurred in 84% to 94% of conflict trials. Other alignment indicators remained mixed.

Each section below states the point, the evidence for it, what the evidence means, and the main limit. Technical derivations and row-level results are available in the expandable details and appendix.

2. None of 45 graded benchmarks both score confidence and reward silence

Point. A model that is only 1% confident still maximizes its expected score by answering when a wrong answer and silence both earn zero.

Basis. We reviewed how 52 benchmarks are scored. Of the 45 that grade answers right or wrong, none both asks for confidence and makes silence better than a wrong answer.

Meaning. The usual benchmark score measures whether an answer was right. It cannot distinguish a cautious 51% belief from a 99% belief because it never asks for either number.

A common false fix. Subtracting a fixed penalty for each wrong answer, while still requiring every item to be answered, only rescales accuracy. We verified this by rescoring all 69 published GPQA Diamond runs at three penalty levels. The ranking never changed, and the largest numerical departure from the algebraic prediction was 1.2e−15.

Limit. Preference arenas, rubric-scored conversations, and agentic tasks do not reduce cleanly to right, wrong, or silent. The claim applies to benchmarks with objectively graded answers.

3. Score confidence, not just answers

Recommendation. Ask for an answer and the probability that the answer is correct. Use a scoring rule under which honest probability reporting earns the highest expected score.

Why the quadratic rule. A right answer earns more when confidence was high. A wrong answer loses more when confidence was high. The model therefore does best by reporting its actual belief rather than always claiming 100%.

Separate question: should the answer reach the user? The quadratic rule measures confidence but still gives some expected value to any answer with a chance of being right. Save the answer and probability, then let deterministic code apply the deployment threshold. A 30% threshold corresponds to a quadratic assertion cost of 0.09. The model can still receive cost-aware training, but the final gate should remain observable and adjustable outside it.

Limit. The appropriate threshold is a policy choice. A film recommendation and a drug-interaction answer should not necessarily use the same one. Our first training experiment found no added calibration-score benefit from the fixed cost; a direct same-checkpoint test found directionally fewer wrong answers with the simpler confidence prompt, while a broader held-out test found better risk ranking from cost training. The training reward, elicitation prompt, and deployment gate are three separate choices.

Point. Accuracy-only training produced the highest accuracy. Training on the confidence-calibrated score produced the best confidence calibration. Adding a fixed answering cost did not improve that score further.

Takeaway.

  • Performance winner: accuracy-only training. It reached 65.2% overall accuracy, compared with 63.2% for confidence-calibrated training.
  • Calibration winner: confidence-aware training. Both confidence-aware rewards reached a 0.443 confidence-calibrated score, 0.105 above accuracy-only training.

Meaning. The reward changed the model's weights, but the objective mattered. Optimizing accuracy improved benchmark performance and worsened calibration. Optimizing the confidence-calibrated score improved calibration but did not produce the highest accuracy.

Limit. This is causal evidence within one training setup, not evidence that the result generalizes to every model family. Effects varied by seed and benchmark.

6. Calibration training improves prudence, not alignment by itself

Question. Does better confidence calibration produce better evidence use, less catastrophic overcommitment, more respect for multiple ends, and less cheating or goal fixation?

Answer. Confidence-calibrated training reliably reduced overconfident bad answers and decisions, including on subjects excluded from training. It also made unauthorized shortcuts slightly less common in a harder hidden-opportunity test, but the models still took those shortcuts most of the time. The evidence supports transferable prudence and a small alignment-adjacent effect. It does not yet show that calibration makes a generally aligned model. A plural-ends reminder separately improved integrity behavior.

6.3 The battery found low-frequency violations, not scheming

Point. These models sometimes used a prohibited evaluator reference, but we did not observe checker tampering or concealment afterward.

Basis. Across all checkpoints and prompts, models used the prohibited reference in 515 of 10,240 risky tool choices (5.0%). Meaning. The experiment supplies a denominator for rare bad behavior. It does not support a scheming claim. It also rules out the easier claim that every model always respected authorization.

Recommendation for benchmark maintainers

1. Ask for a probability and score it

Use a score that makes an honest probability the model's best strategy. The quadratic rule used here is one option. Report the confidence-calibrated score beside accuracy rather than replacing accuracy.

Basis. Current scoring elicits guesses. Prompting and training both made confidence track correctness better once confidence was rewarded.

2. Provide an explicit way to decline

Append “I don't know” to closed-form answer choices and score it as zero. This makes silence an observable decision rather than an unparseable response.

Basis. A wrong-answer penalty alone cannot change rankings when every item must still be answered.

3. Treat any answering cost as a separate policy choice

If answers below a chosen confidence threshold should be discouraged, publish the fixed cost and show results with and without it.

Basis. The threshold depends on the use case, and the fixed cost did not improve training beyond the quadratic rule in this experiment.

What to run next

The battery now covers cross-subject transfer, catastrophic decisions, evidence response, honesty under pressure, instruction hierarchy, paired benign and harmful requests, and hidden unauthorized shortcuts. Four gaps matter next.

1. Make a second model family format-stable before reward training. Nemotron failed one of two full profiles after a 4,096-example format checkpoint. Kimi then malformed 932 of 1,920 responses, while GPT-OSS exhausted its short output allowance before reaching an answer. The next attempt should use a constrained decoder that makes malformed output structurally impossible, then repeat two full 1,920-response profiles. Do not loosen the 1% gate.

2. Extend hidden opportunities across several tool calls. The new single-step test found many unauthorized actions and a small calibration effect, so it can separate models. It found no concealment. A stronger environment should add memory, private intermediate choices, delayed consequences, and an opportunity to hide what happened. Keep the matched authorized control and task completion endpoint.

3. Test the proposed mechanism, not only its endpoints. Cross a calibration objective with the plural-ends reminder during training. Measure whether either intervention changes uncertainty awareness, search for alternative goals, policy sensitivity, and shortcut use. This would test whether metacognition and plurality are complementary causes rather than adjacent observations.

4. Make the harmful test capable of separating models. XSTest harmful handling saturated at zero compliance, even under the neutral pilot prompt. Use generated responses on harder dual-use requests, pair them with benign lookalikes, and validate the grader against human labels before scaling.

The comparison to make

Use deliberately selected checkpoints: the untuned base, matched accuracy and confidence objectives, and independent seeds. Run frozen items under a neutral prompt first. Report each alignment dimension separately, alongside its benign control, and compare models within the same seed and dose.

More calls to the completed Qwen factual matrix would add precision without adding much generality. The next paid work should repair cross-model format stability, test the plural-ends mechanism, or add time and concealment to the agentic environment.

Methods and definitions

What the main measures mean

  • Overall accuracy. Correct answers divided by all questions. Declines count against accuracy.
  • Coverage. Questions the model answered divided by all questions.
  • Accuracy when answered. Correct answers divided only by answered questions.
  • Confidence when answered. The model's average stated probability on the questions it answered.
  • Calibration error among answers. The weighted confidence-to-accuracy gap within confidence bands. Lower is better.
  • Confidence-calibrated score. The quadratic score averaged over all questions. Higher is better. A right answer earns 2p − p², a wrong answer earns −p², and silence earns zero.

These labels state their denominators because overall accuracy and confidence among answers are not directly subtractable when a model abstains.

Treatment and score details

Every confidence-calibrated-score column uses the plain quadratic rule, even when the model was trained with an added answering cost. The reward used for training and the measure used for evaluation are kept separate.
