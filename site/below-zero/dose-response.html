What reward training changes

Five reward objectives, each measured against the untuned model it started from, under two references: plain accuracy and the proposed quadratic rule. They agree about four of the five. Training on accuracy alone raises accuracy while making the model's probabilities worse, which is the trade a benchmark reporting only accuracy cannot see.

What each version is

Four things can vary. Naming them is worth doing carefully, because two of them appear twice in different roles, and conflating those two roles is the most common way to misread this kind of result.

factorrolelevels on this page
1. Scoring rule disclosed in the promptGenerationHeld fixed: none. Every answer here was produced under a neutral prompt, so nothing on this page measures a model reacting to an announced rule.
2. Is a probability requested?GenerationHeld fixed: yes. Every prompt asks for an answer and a probability, so abstention and confidence are always available actions.
3. How the probability enters the scoreTraining rewardIgnored (accuracy only), or quadratic.
3. How the probability enters the scorePost-hoc scoringBinary, quadratic, logarithmic, or spherical. The last two were never used as a reward by any arm.
4. Fixed cost for assertingTraining rewardNone, 0.04, 0.09, or 0.16.
4. Fixed cost for assertingPost-hoc scoringNone, 0.04, 0.09, or 0.16, applied to stored answers.

The distinction in factors 3 and 4 is the one that matters. A rule can be the thing a model was trained to maximise, or the thing its stored answers are scored with afterwards, or the thing it was told about in the prompt. Those are three different questions. This page varies the first, applies the second after the fact, and holds the third fixed at "told nothing".

Two reference measures

Every version is reported under two measures, and the distance between them is the argument this project is making.

Plain accuracy is what benchmarks already report: one point for a right answer, nothing otherwise. The quadratic score is the proposed replacement. It is strictly proper, which is a technical property with a plain meaning: a model gets its best expected score by reporting the probability it actually holds, so there is no confidence it can claim that beats telling the truth.

That property is why nothing else is used here. An earlier draft of this page led with a rule-free measure of how much confidence each version spent on wrong answers. It was intuitive and it was improper: it is minimised by reporting zero confidence on everything, so it rewards a model that answers constantly while claiming to believe nothing. Arguing that benchmarks should adopt a proper rule, using an improper one to do it, would have been the wrong instrument regardless of how the numbers came out.

The two measures disagree about exactly one version, and that disagreement is the result. Accuracy-only training makes the model more accurate and its probabilities worse. It gains 4 to 6 points of accuracy and loses 0.06 to 0.10 of quadratic score at every dose. It bought those points by asserting more often and more confidently, and the proper rule charges for the wrong answers that came with them. A benchmark reporting only accuracy would record that trade as an unambiguous improvement.

Every version that prices confidence improves on both measures at once, at eleven of its twelve measured points. The exception is cost 0.09 at 500 steps, which lands 0.008 below the base on the quadratic rule, a gap an order of magnitude smaller than the one that defines the result. That is the claim worth carrying: pricing confidence does not cost accuracy, and measuring only accuracy hides what accuracy costs.

Among the four confidence-aware versions the differences are small, do not order consistently across doses, and should not be read as a ranking. The split that matters is between the accuracy-only control and the rest.

Scored by rules it never trained on

The logarithmic and spherical columns were never anyone's training objective. The accuracy-only version loses badly under the logarithmic rule, which is the rule that punishes confident errors without limit. That is the same trade the accuracy column shows from the other side. The score scales differ between columns, so compare down a column rather than across a row.

Against no training at all

On behavioural probes, every version is a large improvement over the untuned model, and that gain arrives almost immediately:

modelconstraint violationsinvalid formatabstained
Untuned base44.4%67.7%20.2%
Best 25-step version (Confidence-aware)20.2%3.0%44.4%
Worst 25-step version (Confidence + cost 0.04)24.2%4.0%40.4%

Relative to base, every 25-step version removes citation and grounding violations entirely and cuts scope violations from 80% to roughly a quarter. A paired item bootstrap over 10,000 resamples puts every violation-rate reduction between 20.2 and 24.2 points, with every 95% interval's lower bound above zero. One category moved the wrong way: grader-shortcut behaviour rose from a base rate of 0%.

Most of the base model's failures are truncated reasoning that never reaches the answer fields, so this contrast includes a large format-adherence effect. That effect is part of what fine-tuning does, but it means base and tuned confidence figures are not measured on comparable subsets.

Where later versions accept less wrong pushback, they also accept less correct pushback. That is increased stubbornness, not improved judgement about who to believe. Reporting the first column alone would be the more flattering result and the wrong one.

What this cannot tell you

One training seed, so no interval on this page covers training-seed variation. The split between the accuracy-only control and the confidence-aware versions is large and consistent across three doses, which is the kind of result most likely to replicate. The ordering within the confidence-aware group is not, and should not be quoted.

Three of these versions have since been trained a second time, from a different seed. The directions on this page held; the magnitudes did not, and every figure here is the more favourable of the two runs. Read the numbers on this page as one draw and the replication page for what survived, including the repeat-measurement floor that says which of these differences are large enough to mean anything.

Three doses are shown because adjacent checkpoints in the full grid differ by less than the noise between them. The complete twenty-dose grid is in the downloadable CSV. Choosing a single best checkpoint from it would be fitting the evaluation set, which is why no table here does that.

One 8B model, 100 held-out questions, 33 behavioural probes, 40 pressure items. These are sample sizes for establishing a direction, not for estimating an effect size, and nothing here shows the result transfers to another model family.

Every generation used a neutral prompt, so nothing here measures a model responding to an announced rule. That is the separate experiment on the findings page.

Every figure here is rendered from the committed CSVs in data/processed, one row per version. This is a single training seed, and no checkpoint on this page was selected for winning.
